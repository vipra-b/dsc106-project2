Proposition: Restricting abortion access through clinic closures effectively reduces abortion rates.

FOR the Proposition

Design decisions and rationale:

  • Binning abortion rate change into three ordered categories for choropleth fill. Earnestness: −0.5. We took the continuous % change in abortion rate and bucketed it into three groups: large decrease (≤−20%), moderate decrease (−5% to −20%), and stable/increase (>−5%). Bucketing data is fine. The issue is that we picked these specific cutoffs to make the visual contrast as strong as possible, so Missouri’s −98% drop and South Dakota’s −74% drop end up in the same dark bucket as states that only fell 21%. A huge amount of variation gets hidden, and the map looks way more uniformly dark than it should. A continuous color scale would have shown the real spread, but the categorical version reads cleaner and hits harder.
  • Square-root scaling of clinic count for bubble size. Earnestness: +1. We √-scaled the clinic counts before turning them into bubble sizes, which is just the right way to do area-encoded circles. Without it, California’s 173 clinics would blow up into a bubble that swallows the whole map. The transformation is honest and keeps everything readable while still preserving the rank order. It also happens to help the proposition though, since the darkest states end up with the tiniest bubbles, and the visual co-occurrence of clinic loss and rate decline jumps right out at you.
  • Restricting annotations to states where clinic loss and rate decline co-occurred. Earnestness: −1.5. The five states we annotated were picked specifically because they line up the cleanest between losing clinics and rates going down. States like Arkansas (−33% clinics, +2% rate), South Carolina (−25% clinics, 0% rate), and Kentucky (+100% clinics, +26% rate) are sitting right there on the map but never get called out. This is probably the most deceptive thing in the whole visualization, because it makes the reader think clinic loss reliably predicts rate decline when the data really doesn’t say that. Labeling some counterexamples was an option we considered, but doing that would have basically killed the proposition.
  • Framing the subtitle as a summary statistic (“8 of 12 states”). Earnestness: −1. Our subtitle says “8 of the 12 states that lost abortion clinics saw abortion rates fall.” Technically true, but the framing cherry-picks. Every state that didn’t lose clinics gets left out of the denominator, and 8/12 = 67% only sounds impressive if you ignore that a bunch of light blue states also had no clinic loss. A broader framing was on the table, but we went with this one because it gives the reader a concrete, credible-sounding number that most people aren’t going to dig into.
  • Title written as a causal claim. Earnestness: −1.5. “Fewer abortion clinics means fewer abortions” basically claims causation, and “Restrictions are working” piles a value judgment on top of that. What we actually have is correlation across a single period (2017–2020), with no causal mechanism shown anywhere. A safer title like “States with fewer abortion clinics tend to have lower abortion rates” would have been more honest but a lot less catchy.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design decisions and rationale:

  • Binary thresholds for “few clinics” and “many residents left state.” Earnestness: +1. We defined “few clinics” as ≥80% of counties with no known clinic and “many” travelers as ≥20% getting abortions out of state, which matches the footnote and lets the reader reproduce the 2×2 grid. This sorts states into a clean story that helps persuasion, but any cutoff is somewhat arbitrary. We could have used medians or terciles instead, which would shift states around and make the extremes feel less dramatic.
  • 2×2 bivariate color scheme (four distinct fills). Earnestness: −0.5. A four-color palette puts both dimensions into one choropleth, so the reader can immediately see “high squeeze” states (dark red) versus states with access or low cross-border travel. The palette itself is polarizing though, since the dark red is much more attention-grabbing than the muted blue and gray used for the other categories, which pushes the reader toward reading red states as bad before they engage with the numbers. A more neutral bivariate palette would treat all four quadrants more evenly.
  • Title and subtitle that interpret the pattern (“displace abortions, not demand”). Earnestness: −1.5. The title states “Closing abortion clinics does not reduce abortions. It sends residents out of state to get them,” which hands the reader a causal conclusion before they even look at the map. This goes well beyond what one cross-section can prove, and it strongly steers them toward agreeing with the against proposition. A more neutral subtitle would stick to definitions only but with a weaker narrative hook.
  • Leader annotations (e.g. Missouri, South Dakota) with exact percentages. Earnestness: −0.5. Calling out the most extreme states grounds the argument in real numbers and makes the bivariate encoding memorable. The choice of which states to label is one-sided though, since all five callouts (Missouri, South Dakota, Wyoming, West Virginia, South Carolina) come from the dark-red “high squeeze” category, and none of the other three quadrants get annotations. A more balanced approach would label notable states from each category to let the reader compare.
  • Geographic clustering claim in the subtitle (South and Plains). Earnestness: −1. Pointing out regional clustering directs attention to a “belt” of high-travel, low-access states and makes the pattern feel systematic. If we removed regional language, the colors would carry more weight and communicate the pattern.

Final Reflection

Designing two opposing visualizations from the same dataset made it really obvious how arbitrary a lot of standard design choices actually are. We picked a geospatial map for both sides early on because the data is state-level, and a map immediately gives the reader a sense of seeing the whole country with patterns laid out before they even start looking at numbers. That framing is doing a lot of work, since once you put data on a map, the reader assumes you’re showing them something comprehensive and objective. We leaned on that assumption pretty hard for both sides. Decisions that feel neutral in isolation, like which states to annotate, where to put a threshold, how to bin a continuous variable, or what verb to use in a title, turned out to be the main levers of persuasion. The most surprising thing was how little we actually had to transform the data to flip the story. The for-prop and against-prop visualizations use almost the same raw columns and tell contradictory stories, which made us realize that a lot of what we’d call analysis is really just rhetorical framing.

The hardest part was making the deception subtle instead of obvious. Our strongest deceptive moves, like selectively annotating only the states that support the proposition, writing causal titles from correlational data, and picking thresholds that maximize quadrant contrast, are also things we see all the time in legitimate data journalism. That blurred the line between persuasion and misleading in ways that were genuinely uncomfortable to think about. We ended up thinking of the distinction less as a binary and more as a spectrum tied to intent and transparency. A design choice feels more acceptable when the transformation is out in the open (like the threshold disclosed in our footnote) and more misleading when it’s hidden (like our selective annotations, which a reader would never catch unless they pulled the underlying numbers themselves).

After doing this, we’d define ethical analysis and visualization as requiring the designer to be able to articulate every transformation and framing choice, and to make sure those choices don’t actively hide information that would reverse the reader’s interpretation. By that standard, the for-prop visualization pushes pretty close to the line. The map shows all 50 states but only annotates the ones where clinic loss and rate decline co-occur, so a reader who trusts the map has no way of knowing they should look for the counterexamples sitting right there in the data (Oklahoma, Kentucky, Mississippi). The against-prop visualization stays closer to the acceptable side. The bivariate pattern it shows is real, the geographic clustering is genuine, and the title’s claim is directionally supported by the data even though we state it with more confidence than the evidence strictly warrants.
